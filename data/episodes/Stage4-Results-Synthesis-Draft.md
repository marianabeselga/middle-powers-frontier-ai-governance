# Working draft: Results section (Stage 4 synthesis)

**Status.** This is a working draft for researcher review, not a frozen final Results chapter. It reorganises the already-coded, frozen analytical corpus (four Episodes, six country profiles, the Stage 4 Cross-Case Matrix, and the Stage 5 relational datasets) into a continuous, RQ-structured empirical argument. No frozen record was modified to produce it; no new evidence was introduced; no substantive coding decision was reopened. Where the underlying corpus itself flags an unresolved question, that question is carried forward explicitly rather than resolved here (see "Claims requiring researcher review," at the end).

**Proposed title:**

> **Configuration, Not Scale: How Middle-Power Coordination Converts Fragmented Capabilities into Governance Leverage over Frontier AI**

---

## 4. Results

The four coordination Episodes examined in this study — the Bletchley–Seoul–Paris–New Delhi summit sequence, the International Network of AI Safety Institutes, the Hiroshima AI Process and its Friends Group, and the Global Partnership on AI — together offer a comparative window onto a question that a single case cannot answer: whether, and under what conditions, coordination among middle powers converts fragmented technical, regulatory, market, and diplomatic capabilities into governance leverage over frontier AI. The Results that follow are organised around the study's four Research Questions rather than around the four Episodes individually. Each Episode recurs throughout the four sections below as comparative evidence for a specific claim, not as a self-contained narrative to be read in sequence. Read together, the four sections build cumulatively: RQ1 establishes what six middle powers bring to the table and how unevenly they bring it; RQ2 shows that bringing capabilities to the table is not the same as coordinating them, and maps where genuine coordination is actually found; RQ3 traces which institutional instruments converted that coordination into observable governance effects, and which did not; and RQ4 draws the first three sections together to ask, empirically, under what conditions coordination generated governance leverage at all, and where the process broke down. Consistent with the study's design, RQ4 is treated here only through its empirical component — the conditions under which leverage was observed — and not through its implications for the consolidation of an international regime or the emergence of enforceable AI red lines, which belong to a later Discussion.

Two disciplines run through every section. First, capability possession is not capability mobilisation, participation is not coordination, coordination is not institutionalisation, and institutionalisation is not governance leverage — the four analytical distinctions the Codebook builds the entire coding scheme around, and which the Results preserve throughout rather than collapsing under narrative pressure. Second, the unit of comparison throughout is the coordination configuration — a specific, named set of actors and the instrument or practice they jointly produced — not the country. Countries appear in what follows as actors within these configurations and as the subject of an attribution layer distinguished carefully from the process-level findings; they are never rank-ordered, scored, or aggregated into a composite measure of national influence.

### 4.1 RQ1 — Configuration: capabilities and structural conditions

*What capabilities and structural conditions position middle powers as potential sources of influence in frontier AI governance?*

The six countries examined — Brazil, Canada, India, Japan, South Korea, and Singapore — do not present a single hierarchy of national capability, and the finalized Stage 1 country profiles do not support constructing one. What they present instead is a set of distinct configurations across five capability dimensions (technical expertise, regulatory capacity, market resources, institutional and diplomatic resources, and evaluation and safety expertise) and four structural conditions (compute and infrastructure access, market size and dependence, geopolitical alignments, and regulatory and institutional arrangements). Four of the six countries — Canada, India, South Korea, and Singapore — show a capability profile that is Strong on four or five of the five dimensions, but the resemblance among them ends there: Canada's profile is qualified by a comparatively small domestic market even as its investment volume and firm base remain internationally significant; India's is Moderate rather than Strong on regulatory capacity and evaluation and safety expertise, reflecting a governance architecture (the 2025–2026 AI Governance Guidelines, an AI Safety Institute announced in 2025) that is real but still consolidating rather than mature; and Singapore's Strong profile rests on regulatory and assurance institutions and a mature voluntary-framework ecosystem rather than on a comprehensive binding statute of the kind Japan and South Korea have each enacted. Japan's profile is Strong on technical expertise, market resources, institutional and diplomatic resources, and evaluation and safety expertise, but only Moderate on regulatory capacity — its 2025 AI Promotion Act is a binding law already in force, yet its light-touch, coordination-based design (no mandatory risk classification or pre-market approval) places its regulatory apparatus a tier below Canada's or South Korea's more enforcement-oriented architectures. Brazil presents the most differentiated configuration of the six: Strong on market resources and on institutional and diplomatic resources — the latter grounded in its 2024–2025 G20 and BRICS presidencies — but only Moderate on technical expertise and regulatory capacity, and Limited on evaluation and safety expertise, reflecting the absence of a dedicated national AI Safety Institute and of membership in the International Network of AI Safety Institutes.

Structural conditions vary less by level than by composition. Every one of the six countries carries some form of external technological dependence — the evidence base treats this as a near-universal feature of the current AI infrastructure landscape rather than as evidence that any one country is structurally exceptional — but the *specific* dependence differs: Brazil's centres on semiconductor and hardware imports; Canada's on foreign-owned cloud and data-centre capacity; Japan's on a foreign-hyperscaler-dependent cloud market and NVIDIA-designed supercomputing infrastructure; India's on foreign-manufactured GPUs even as its own chip-fabrication programme scales up; and Singapore's, most explicitly, on a government-acknowledged inability "to compete with major powers in assembling raw computing power," addressed through leasing and chipmaker partnerships rather than domestic capacity alone. South Korea is the only one of the six coded Enabling rather than Mixed on compute and infrastructure access, on the strength of quantified, primary-source evidence of rapidly scaling domestic GPU capacity (a Gwangju facility already operating 880 H100 GPUs, with a planned national supercomputer and an announced longer-term target exceeding 260,000 GPUs) — a difference in the *documented scale and trajectory* of infrastructure build-out, not a claim that Korea has achieved technological autonomy from the same NVIDIA-led global supply chain every other case also depends on. On geopolitical alignment and on regulatory and institutional arrangements, five of the six countries are coded Enabling; only Brazil's regulatory arrangements are coded Mixed, reflecting a genuine coexistence of an advanced data-protection and AI-strategy architecture with the continued absence, as of the observation period, of a comprehensive AI statute in force.

The central safeguard the Codebook builds into this baseline, and that the Results preserve throughout, is that **capability possession does not establish capability mobilisation**. A country's Stage 1 profile records resources available for mobilisation, not resources actually deployed in any specific coordination process; the methodological validation exercise conducted on the first three Bletchley cases confirmed that this distinction discriminates cleanly even where every country involved carries a Strong capability profile — in Brazil's, Canada's, and India's Bletchley-episode records alike, a uniformly Strong or near-Strong Stage 1 profile translated into documented within-episode mobilisation on only one dimension (institutional and diplomatic resources, C4), and even then only at specific, named, dated moments rather than as a general inference from the baseline rating. The clearest illustration of why this distinction cannot be relaxed comes from comparing Brazil's two most technically demanding coordination opportunities: within the Bletchley sequence, no evidence shows Brazil's Stage 1 technical capability being deployed in any documented joint activity, while within GPAI, named Brazilian experts are independently confirmed as active contributors to at least one Expert Working Group. The same underlying capability profile therefore appears mobilised in one governance arena and not in another — a pattern more consistent with each Episode offering a different *opportunity structure* for mobilisation than with any change in Brazil's own capability, and a caution against reading a single Episode's mobilisation pattern as evidence about a country's capability as such.

### 4.2 RQ2 — Coordination mechanisms: mobilisation and coordination

*How are these capabilities mobilised and combined through coordination among middle powers?*

If RQ1 establishes what the six countries bring, RQ2 asks how much of it was actually combined through coordination — and the central finding is that **participation was substantially broader than established coordination**. Across the four Episodes, the six countries' documented participation, membership, endorsement, or institutional access reaches a bipartite network density of between 0.875 and 0.917 (the lower figure excludes Brazil's weaker, observer-tier presence at one Hiroshima Friends Group meeting): participation, in other words, is close to universal. Established coordination — coded, per the Codebook's Critical Rule, only where the evidence documents an actual interaction, exchange, joint activity, or deliberate combination of capabilities, and never inferred from co-attendance, shared membership, hosting, or co-signature alone — is a different picture entirely. The direct, dyadic actor-to-actor coordination network contains exactly two genuinely bilateral ties across all four Episodes (Singapore–Japan within Bletchley; Japan–India within Hiroshima), for a network density of 0.133. This gap between participation and coordination is not a modelling artefact; it is the expected signature of a coding scheme built specifically to prevent participation from being read as coordination, and its scale is itself informative about how demanding that evidentiary bar is in practice.

The gap, however, is not uniform across the four Episodes, and its variation is one of the more analytically useful patterns the network representation adds to the process-tracing findings already on record. In the Bletchley sequence, four of the six participating countries — Singapore, Japan, India, and Canada — reach at least one established coordination tie, while Brazil and South Korea do not: a **selective conversion**. In the AISI Network, every one of the four participating countries (Singapore, Japan, South Korea, and Canada; Brazil and India have no documented presence in this Episode at all) is tied into the established Joint Testing Exercises configuration — a **universal conversion within relevant participants**, though one that still conceals an internal leadership/contributor distinction discussed below. In the Hiroshima AI Process, only Japan and India clear the coordination threshold, while Canada, South Korea, Singapore, and Brazil (whose strongest documented tie is an observer role at a single later meeting) do not: the **most selective** conversion of the four, and the widest participation–coordination gap in the study. In GPAI, all six countries reach at least one established tie, but the network is **universal and stratified**: every country is tied into at least one Expert Working Group, in which participating individuals act, on the evidence of the Working Groups' own published material, with full independence of their governments, while only three countries — Canada, Japan, and India — additionally reach a state-level founding, hosting, or chairmanship tie. GPAI's uniform actor-level inclusion therefore masks a tier-based selectivity that a simple participation/coordination binary would miss.

Three distinct network representations make different parts of this pattern visible, and it is worth being precise about what each one can and cannot support, since network position is not treated in this study as evidence of power, influence, or brokerage capacity — only as structural evidence about how coordination is organised.

**Representation A — participation and access** (Figure 2) is a bipartite graph of six actors against four Episodes, recording documented involvement of any kind. Its purpose is to make the scale of the participation–coordination gap visible at a glance, Episode by Episode, without collapsing the four distinct patterns described above into one aggregate ratio.

**Representation B — direct actor-to-actor coordination** is limited, deliberately, to the two genuinely dyadic relationships the evidence supports (Singapore–Japan; Japan–India). **Representation C — actor-to-configuration coordination** (Figure 3) connects the six actors to seven named MECHANISM, COALITION, and INSTRUMENT hub nodes — the New Delhi Science Working Group, the AISI Network's Joint Testing Exercises, the Hiroshima Friends Group, the GPAI Founding Coalition, the GPAI–OECD Integrated Partnership, and the two GPAI Expert Working Groups — through the 22 established coordination edges the Codebook's own node typology supports. This is the representation that carries most of the study's established coordination findings, precisely because most of them involve three or more actors jointly attached to a single named process rather than a clean bilateral exchange. Shared attachment to one of these hubs is not read as evidence of a direct tie between the attached actors: no one-mode projection was computed from this structure, because doing so would manufacture, for example, a Brazil–Canada relationship from nothing more than co-membership in the same GPAI Working Group roster — exactly the inference the Codebook's Critical Rule exists to prevent.

Read against the Cross-Case Matrix's earlier process-tracing findings, the network evidence corroborates and sharpens — rather than contradicts — the recurring pattern of **selective sub-coalitions inside broad forums**: the New Delhi Science Working Group (Singapore, India, Canada) inside the much larger Bletchley sequence, and the Japan–India bilateral channel inside the much larger Hiroshima Friends Group, are both instances of a small, specifically named subset of actors achieving demonstrable coordination within a nominal membership several times its size. GPAI's Expert Working Groups complicate a simple restatement of this pattern, because *all six* study cases reach the Working Group tier — the selectivity in GPAI operates on tie type (state versus expert) rather than on which actors are included at all. The AISI Network is the clean exception: its one established configuration already spans its full four-country participating membership, with no narrower sub-coalition nested inside it.

**Singapore** offers the clearest instance of a country reaching an established tie repeatedly across Episodes: it holds a leadership-type role — co-lead of the AISI Network's Testing and Evaluation Track, co-chair of the New Delhi Science Working Group, co-chair of one GPAI Expert Working Group — in three of the four Episodes, the sole exception being Hiroshima, where its participation does not clear the coordination threshold at all. This pattern should not be read as evidence of a general Singaporean structural characteristic in middle-power AI coordination: two of Singapore's four ties — the Bletchley-episode Singapore–Japan joint testing exercise and the AISI Network's own Joint Testing Exercises — are explicitly described in the frozen Bletchley record as conducted "under the AISI network," meaning they plausibly reflect one underlying institutional relationship observed across two Episode boundaries rather than two fully independent observations. Singapore's evidentiary base is accordingly closer to two-and-a-half independent instances than three, a qualification the underlying network-analysis memo itself makes explicit and that this synthesis preserves rather than smooths over.

**Institutional entrepreneurship** — a single state constructing, expanding, or chairing a governance arrangement — recurs across the four Episodes, but it recurs as at least three structurally distinct forms, not as one repeatable pattern with interchangeable instances, and the three most commonly cited examples (Korea at Bletchley, Japan at Hiroshima, India at GPAI) should not be treated as equivalent. Korea's Seoul host-authorship never clears "plausible, approaching established" in the frozen Bletchley record, and consequently has **no representation at all** in the established-coordination network — a materially weaker evidentiary position than the other two. Japan's construction of the Hiroshima Friends Group is a genuine **singleton attachment**: Japan alone is tied to that node, with no second actor sharing the specific established-coordination finding, and the tie is more evidentially secure than Korea's precisely because the Friends Group's founding, expansion, and dated Action Plan are documented as an actual, completed institution-building act rather than inferred from Korea's own retrospective characterisation of its role in a jointly signed text. India's role in the 2024 GPAI–OECD Integrated Partnership is structurally identical in shape to Japan's — a second true singleton attachment — but substantively different in kind: India's act integrates two pre-existing institutions rather than constructing a new one from nothing. A fourth, related configuration surfaces only in the network representation itself: Canada's and Japan's shared status as GPAI founders attaches both countries to the same "GPAI Founding Coalition" hub, but no documented tie connects Canada and Japan to each other specifically — each is independently one of fifteen founders, a multi-actor attachment with no established inter-actor tie, distinct again from both Japan's and India's singleton patterns and from Korea's absence of any established tie whatsoever.

### 4.3 RQ3 — Institutional instruments: conversion into governance leverage

*Through which institutional instruments does coordinated action convert middle-power capabilities into governance leverage over frontier AI?*

Where RQ2 mapped how coordination was organised, RQ3 asks what happened once it was. The Codebook's analytical sequence — capabilities mobilised, combined through a coordination mechanism, structured through an institutional instrument, and converted into governance leverage — is a framework for reconstructing each Episode's process, not a claim that every Episode follows it in a single, uninterrupted line; the frozen Episode records diverge from strict linearity in specific, informative ways (Canada's Bletchley-episode coordination with France ran parallel to, rather than derived from, the multilateral declarations it also joined; GPAI's capability mobilisation itself bifurcates into a state-level and an expert-level track that never merge). Applied comparatively across the four Episodes, this framework identifies four main types of institutional conversion at work: technical testing and evaluation (the AISI Network's joint exercises, and the Singapore–Japan bilateral exercise nested within Bletchley); standards and information infrastructure (the GPAI Expert Working Groups' recurring reports and tools); institutional architecture-building (Japan's Hiroshima Friends Group, the GPAI founding coalition and its 2024 integration with the OECD, and the trilateral New Delhi Science Working Group); and developer-facing commitments and reporting (the Bletchley sequence's Frontier AI Safety and Impact Commitments, and the Hiroshima AI Process's OECD Reporting Framework).

| Episode | Institutionalisation | Main established governance leverage |
|---|---|---|
| Bletchley | Level 2 — Organised/repeated | GL1–GL3 established; GL5 at moderate confidence |
| AISI Network | Level 4 — Operationalised | GL1–GL3 established |
| Hiroshima | Level 3–4 — Formalised approaching Operationalised | GL1–GL3 established; **GL5 established** |
| GPAI | Level 4 — Operationalised | GL1 and GL3 established; GL2 plausible |

The comparison this table makes visible is not that more institutionalisation produces more leverage. The two Episodes reaching the highest institutionalisation level observed in the study — the AISI Network and GPAI, both Level 4 — are precisely the two that do *not* reach a regulatory or compliance-type effect (GL4 or GL5), while Bletchley, institutionalised only to Level 2, reaches GL5 at moderate confidence, and Hiroshima, institutionalised to Level 3–4, reaches GL5 as an established finding — the strongest and most explicit compliance-type effect identified across all four Episodes. The pattern the comparison instead supports, tentatively, is that **the type and governance location of the institutional instrument appear more consequential for the strongest observed forms of leverage than institutionalisation level alone.** Bletchley and Hiroshima each institutionalised a developer-facing instrument — a signed commitment, in Bletchley's case; a live, OECD-administered reporting mechanism with published transparency reports, in Hiroshima's — that gave a compliance-type effect a concrete channel through which to be observed. The AISI Network and GPAI institutionalised only government-institute-facing or expert-facing outputs — joint testing reports, Working Group tools — with no comparable channel. This is offered, consistent with the Cross-Case Matrix's own framing, as a comparative pattern visible across four cases, not as a universal causal law; the developer-facing/not distinction is itself confounded, in this small set, with the more diplomatically-anchored character of the two Episodes that reach GL5, so the current evidence cannot fully separate "instrument type" from "type of governance arena" as the operative variable.

Three qualifications, specific to individual Episodes, are worth stating precisely rather than folded into the general pattern. At **Bletchley**, GL5 represents a formal commitment and compliance effect — thirteen leading AI developers signing the New Delhi Frontier AI Impact Commitments, following the earlier Frontier AI Safety Commitments at Seoul — and should be read as exactly that: documented, dated changes in developers' disclosed commitments, not as confirmed operational behavioural change on the ground, which the available evidence does not independently verify. At **Hiroshima**, by contrast, GL5 rests on stronger evidence of an actual behavioural or practice effect: independent downstream analysis of organisations reporting under the OECD framework found "increasingly sophisticated practices," a documented change in disclosed practice quality over time rather than only a signature event. At the **AISI Network**, technical and epistemic coordination generated genuine institutional, standards, and information effects — recurring, cumulative joint testing exercises; a growing set of evaluation-methodology consensus points; actual cross-lingual benchmark data — but no established regulatory or compliance effect, a limitation the evidence treats as a function of the configuration's own instrumental scope (a testing network has no obvious channel to a regulatory or compliance outcome) rather than a failure of coordination. At **GPAI**, durable institutional and information effects are likewise established — most visibly the 2024 integration with the OECD — but no GL4 or GL5 finding is established, and this is not an inference from silence: the underlying material explicitly and repeatedly declines to attribute specific national regulatory developments, including South Korea's AI Basic Act, to GPAI's own coordination, a disciplined negative finding this synthesis preserves rather than overrides. Governance leverage of type GL5 should not, in any of the four Episodes, be read as evidence of enforcement capacity: the Codebook draws a firm line between a documented compliance-type consequence and any claim that the underlying coordination process can compel or verify compliance, and none of the four Episodes' evidence extends to the latter claim.

### 4.4 RQ4 — Governance leverage: conditions and breaks

*Under what conditions does middle-power coordination generate governance leverage?*

Drawing the previous three sections together, the evidence across the four Episodes is consistent with governance leverage appearing where five conditions co-occur: an identifiable relational coordination configuration (not participation alone); mobilisation of a relevant capability within that specific configuration (not merely its Stage 1 availability); a connection to a concrete institutional instrument or practice that the configuration actually produced; an instrument located at a governance point capable of carrying a consequential effect; and an identifiable governance effect observable at the process level. Where all five hold — most completely in Hiroshima's Japan–India channel feeding into the OECD Reporting Framework, and at the process level in Bletchley's New Delhi-stage instruments — governance leverage of some kind is established. Where one or more conditions is missing, the evidence is equally informative about where, specifically, the process stopped.

Five distinct break patterns recur across the four Episodes, and none of them should be generalised into a universal causal law from a sample of four cases. **Participation without established coordination** is the most common: Brazil and South Korea in Bletchley, and four of six countries in Hiroshima, participated extensively without the evidence clearing the coordination threshold. **Institutional access without relational coordination** describes Canada's and Singapore's Hiroshima Friends Group membership specifically — genuine, founding-level access that never converts into a documented joint activity with a named partner. **Coordination that did not generate a consequential institutional output** describes Canada's plausible, but still prospective, bilateral research channel with France inside Bletchley: a named joint committee exists, but no completed output is documented. **Institutional activity producing information or standards effects without regulatory or behavioural effects** is GPAI's and the AISI Network's shared signature: both reach GL1 and GL3 through genuine, multi-year operational activity, and both stop, cleanly and by the evidence's own account, before GL4 or GL5. **Broad multilateral participation without an established middle-power-specific coordination configuration** describes the six-case "bloc" the underlying research material sometimes asserts for GPAI: no evidence distinguishes the six study cases as a separable coalition within GPAI's roughly 140-member Working Groups, even though each is individually, and genuinely, coordinating within the broader multilateral body.

### 4.5 Ex ante analytical expectations

Three propositions were formulated from the study's theoretical framework before the four Episodes were compared, and are assessed here on their own original terms — not reworded to fit the comparative findings more comfortably, and kept analytically distinct from the emergent, inductively derived propositions the cross-case comparison itself suggests (recorded in `Stage4-Cross-Case-Matrix.md`, §11, and not restated as findings here).

**Proposition 1 (collective leverage): partially supported.** Where capability combination is adjudicable at all — that is, within the relational, multi-actor coordination configurations rather than the unilateral institutional-entrepreneurship acts — it consistently co-occurs with some collective governance leverage. But the proposition's implicit scaling claim, that a greater degree of combination produces stronger leverage, is not borne out: GPAI's Expert Working Groups are the broadest combination of capabilities and positions examined in this study, spanning the largest number of countries of any configuration, and yet stop short of GL4 or GL5, while Hiroshima's much narrower configurations — a single bilateral channel, plus one state's unilateral institution-building act — belong to the one Episode that reaches every governance-leverage category the Codebook defines. Position compatibility, meanwhile, is declaratively high across nearly every country in every Episode, including Brazil's in Bletchley, where no coordination is established at all; it does not discriminate outcomes on its own.

**Proposition 2 (institutionalisation): partially supported, with the qualification already developed in §4.3.** Institutionalisation of some form is associated with informational and institutional-uptake effects (GL1 and GL3) across all four Episodes without exception. It does not track the strongest compliance-type effect: the two Episodes reaching the highest institutionalisation level do not reach it, while two less institutionalised Episodes do. Durability itself — the proposition's own language — remains difficult to adjudicate on the present evidence: all four Episodes are recent (2022–2026), and none of the coded governance-leverage findings has been observed over an extended window.

**Proposition 3 (structural contingency): not adjudicable on current evidence.** Of the structural mechanisms the proposition names — technological and market dependencies, geopolitical relationships, regulatory and institutional environments, and dependence on external AI-technology providers — only geopolitical alignment (S3) and regulatory/institutional arrangements (S4) were coded at all within the four Episodes, and both were carried forward as contextual background rather than independently traced through to a specific coordination or governance-leverage outcome. Technological and market dependency and external-infrastructure reliance do not appear as coded variables in any of the four Episodes' frozen material. This is recorded here as an evidence limitation, not as a negative finding about structural contingency itself: several named mechanisms were simply not coded at the granularity the proposition would require to be tested.

### 4.6 Integrated cross-case synthesis

Taken together, the four Episodes support a **configuration-based rather than scale-based account of collective governance leverage**. Capabilities matter throughout — every established coordination configuration this study identifies rests on some combination of technical, regulatory, market, diplomatic, or evaluative resources documented at Stage 1 — but they matter as potential sources of influence whose mobilisation is neither automatic nor uniform: the same Stage 1 profile can be mobilised in one Episode and not in another, and mobilisation itself is a documented, dated act, never inferred from a baseline rating. Whether mobilised capabilities become coordination depends on relational configuration, not on participation: across four Episodes and six countries, the gap between near-universal participation and comparatively rare established coordination is the single most consistent empirical pattern in the study, and it recurs, in different proportions, in every Episode examined. Where coordination is established, its conversion into an observable governance effect depends on producing a concrete institutional instrument or practice, and the strongest observed forms of that effect appear more closely tied to the *type and governance location* of the instrument produced — whether it reaches a developer-facing point of leverage — than to how formally or how extensively the underlying coordination was institutionalised. And broader coalitions do not, on this evidence, automatically produce stronger leverage: GPAI's broad multilateral Expert Working Groups and Hiroshima's narrow bilateral channel occupy opposite ends of the breadth spectrum, yet it is the narrower configuration that reaches the fuller range of governance-leverage categories.

This account is offered with the qualifications the evidence itself imposes, not as a general theory of middle-power coordination. The study rests on four Episodes; complementarity and capability combination are adjudicable only where relational coordination is independently established, which leaves the unilateral institutional-entrepreneurship findings — a substantial share of the study's positive coordination results — outside the scope of that particular test. All four Episodes are recent, and none offers a long observation window against which durability, in Proposition 2's sense, can be assessed. The type-of-instrument variable identified in §4.3 as more consequential than institutionalisation level is, in this four-Episode set, confounded with the diplomatic-versus-technical character of the governance arena in which each instrument arose, and the current evidence cannot fully separate the two. Structural contingency (Proposition 3) remains an evidence limitation rather than an adjudicated finding. None of these qualifications should be read as licence to claim more than the evidence establishes: the Results answer the study's Main Question only to the extent the four Episodes actually support — that coordination among middle powers can, under specific, identifiable relational and institutional conditions, convert fragmented capabilities into observable collective governance leverage, and that where those conditions are absent, coordination either does not form or stalls before producing an observable effect. What this pattern implies for the consolidation of a broader international regime, or for the emergence and operationalisation of global AI red lines, is a question this Results section deliberately leaves open for the Discussion that follows it.

## Claims requiring researcher review

The items below were carried forward from explicit flags already present in the frozen analytical corpus itself when this draft was first produced. None was used to change any figure, table, or finding above, and none should be read as an error in the underlying coding — each was presented as the source material itself presents it. Items 1–3 have since been reviewed by the researcher (2026-09-07); items 4–5 were surfaced for awareness and confirmed as out of scope for this Results stage, not overlooked.

1. **Unimplemented methodological recommendation on Bletchley's Korea and Japan M findings.** `Stage4-Cross-Case-Matrix.md` §6 records that a read-only methodological review of the Bletchley episode (conducted after all six countries were processed) recommended downgrading both Korea's host-authorship coordination finding and Japan's forum-linkage coordination finding to "not established," on the grounds that Korea's evidence rests on self-characterisation and Japan's on a unilateral, non-relational act. That recommendation was never written into the frozen Bletchley Episode record, because the review itself was explicitly read-only; the frozen file still codes Korea at "plausible, approaching established" and Japan at "plausible, not established." This synthesis reports the frozen coding throughout (consistent with the instruction not to reopen frozen coding). **Researcher review (2026-09-07): the frozen coding for both Korea and Japan is intentionally left unchanged — neither finding is downgraded. The documented recommendation is preserved as an open methodological/interpretive issue for the Discussion stage, not reopened here, and the gap between the recommendation and the coding in force is treated as an audit-trail item, not as an error requiring correction. No dataset value was changed.**

2. **Singapore–Japan tie: cross-Episode, not same-Episode, corroboration.** The Singapore–Japan joint testing exercise within the Bletchley episode is coded as established coordination on the strength of Singapore's own, independently verified Bletchley-era Research Note; this same activity is not corroborated within Japan's own country-attribution section of the same Episode file. `Stage5-Dataset1-Participation-Access.md` and `Stage5-Dataset2-Established-Coordination.md` (Table 2A note) both flag this explicitly, and it has not been treated as grounds to downgrade the established finding. **Researcher review (2026-09-07): the precise evidentiary picture is — Bletchley-era evidence: Singapore's own Research Note (`EVD-SGP-015`); later corroboration: `EP-AISI-NETWORK-2024.md` independently documents this same activity as the Network's own Feb. 2025 second Joint Testing Exercise; Japan's own Bletchley country-attribution record: silent throughout. This is classified explicitly as cross-Episode corroboration (`corroboration_scope = cross_episode`), not same-Episode corroboration by Japan — the AISI Network's later record does not change what Japan's own Bletchley note does or does not say. The relationship is not downgraded merely because Japan's Bletchley record is silent, and Japan's Bletchley M finding is not upgraded as a consequence of this corroboration (see item 1). No targeted primary-source check was requested; no coding change was made.** See also item 3, which addresses the separate question of how the two related raw observations should be counted.

3. **Possible duplicate among two of Singapore's five raw established-coordination observations.** `Stage5-Network-Analysis-Memo.md` notes explicitly that the Bletchley-episode Singapore–Japan joint testing exercise and the AISI Network's own Joint Testing Exercises configuration are described, in the frozen Bletchley record itself, as conducted "under the AISI network" — meaning they may reflect one underlying relationship rather than two independent raw observations. **Researcher review (2026-09-07) — resolved by formal tagging, not merged rows: `Stage5-Dataset2-Established-Coordination.md` now tags both raw observations with a shared `underlying_relationship_id` (`REL-SGP-JPN-AISI-TESTING-01`) and a `possible_cross_episode_duplicate: true` flag, and adds a "Deduplicated relationship count" note distinguishing Singapore's 5 raw observations (Table 2A: 1; Table 2B: 4) from the 4 distinct underlying relationships they correspond to once the flagged pair is counted once, across the same 3 Episodes. Both original raw observations, and every other row, are otherwise unchanged; no evidence or episode attribution was altered; Japan's own Bletchley M finding was not upgraded by this tagging (see item 1). The raw count of 5 remains correct for observation-level reporting; the deduplicated count of 4 should be used when the text refers to distinct or independent underlying relationships — the two should not be conflated, and neither replaces the other. See `Coding-Decisions-Log.md`, 2026-09-07, for the full audit-trail entry.**
