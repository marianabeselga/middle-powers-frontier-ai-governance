# EVD-KOR-002 — Korea's AI regulatory/institutional landscape: AI Basic Act (binding law in force) and the broader tracked-instrument landscape

## Evidence (from Source)

The Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and the Creation of a Foundation for Trust (Act No. 20676) was newly enacted 21 January 2025 and became enforceable 22 January 2026 — a genuinely **binding law now in force**. It defines "high-impact AI" by a list of high-risk domains (energy, drinking water, health/medical devices, nuclear materials, biometric/criminal-investigation use, employment/credit decisions, transportation systems, public-service eligibility decisions, student evaluation) and imposes risk-management, transparency/labelling, safety, verification/certification, and impact-assessment obligations on operators of high-impact and generative AI. It applies extraterritorially to conduct impacting the domestic market, requiring foreign operators to designate a domestic agent. It also establishes, on a statutory basis, the National AI Committee (under the President), the AI Policy Center, and the AI Safety Institute (see [EVD-KOR-003](EVD-KOR-003.md), [EVD-KOR-008](EVD-KOR-008.md)).

Regulations.ai's tracker records 22 instruments for Korea. Only three are binding Acts currently In Force: the AI Basic Act itself, the amended Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA, 2023 — data-subject rights re automated decisions, up to 10% of revenue in maximum penalties for serious violations), and the Act on the Development and Supply of Intelligent Robots (2008, Amended). Every AI-specific horizontal bill proposed before the AI Basic Act was withdrawn or remains merely proposed (7 bills, 2020–2023, all Withdrawn except one still Proposed). The remainder of the tracked landscape consists of policies (National AI Basic Plan 2026–2028, 2019 National Strategy, 2022 Digital Strategy, 2023 Digital Bill of Rights) and non-binding sectoral guidelines (Generative AI Service User Protection Guideline, AI Security Guide, several PIPC guidance documents on automated decisions and AI privacy risk management, National AI Ethics Standards).

## Constructs supported

- **C2 — Regulatory expertise/capacity:** the AI Basic Act's risk-tiered, extraterritorial structure — materially more comprehensive than Japan's light-touch Act No. 53 (see [EVD-JPN-002](../02-Evidence/EVD-JPN-002.md)) — combined with the PIPA's own detailed automated-decision provisions and substantial penalty regime, supports **C2 — C-3 (Strong)**. The one-year grace period for administrative fines/corrective orders (noted in the Research Note) is treated as an implementation-phase detail, not as evidence against regulatory capacity itself.
- **S4 — Regulatory/institutional arrangements:** the combination of a binding, comprehensive horizontal Act, a multi-body institutional architecture (MSIT, National AI Committee, PIPC, KCC, KISA, KIRIA — see [EVD-KOR-003](EVD-KOR-003.md)), and a dense layer of sectoral/technical guidelines provides multiple well-developed channels for AI governance coordination, supporting **S4 — Enabling**.

## Notes

**Law-in-force distinction (critical):** Korea's AI Basic Act is the most comprehensive binding, currently-enforceable horizontal AI statute identified across the six cases processed so far — structurally closer to the EU AI Act's risk-tiered model than Japan's coordination-based Act No. 53, and materially different in legal status from Brazil's pending bill and Canada's withdrawn bill. This distinction is preserved explicitly here and in [CTY-KOR.md](../00-Countries/CTY-KOR.md).
