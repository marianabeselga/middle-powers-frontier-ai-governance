# EVD-KOR-005 — OECD: firm-level AI adoption, semiconductor export weight, and external supply-chain dependence

## Evidence (from Source)

OECD analyses report Korean AI adoption of 36% among large firms and 28% among SMEs (regional-development report, 2025); a separate 2025 labour-market report reports 31% of surveyed Korean SMEs had adopted generative AI, and elsewhere in the same report, 27.4% adoption among firms with 50–249 employees versus 63.3% among firms with more than 250 employees. The OECD Economic Surveys: Korea 2024 documents that semiconductor exports are a major driver of Korean economic growth, that the economy depends heavily on goods exports with geographically/product-concentrated exports, and that approximately 21% of imported items for materials, parts, and equipment depended on China for more than 50% of supply in 2022. The Research Note also references the OECD's 2026 Korea Survey confirming that surging semiconductor exports continue to drive growth, indicating this is not a one-off 2024 finding.

## Constructs supported

- **C3 — Market resources:** established firm-level AI adoption (large firms substantially ahead of SMEs) combined with a globally significant, export-oriented semiconductor/manufacturing base corroborates [EVD-KOR-001](EVD-KOR-001.md) and [EVD-KOR-004](EVD-KOR-004.md), supporting **C3 — C-3 (Strong)**.
- **S1 — Compute/infrastructure:** assessed against Codebook §16's full definition ("access to, dependence on, or constraints concerning compute, cloud services, models, chips, infrastructure, or related technological resources"), distinguishing four dimensions (per the 2026-09-02 S1 calibration):
  - *Domestic infrastructure and compute capacity:* "highly developed digital infrastructure" and "a strong position in semiconductor production" (OECD); corroborated by Korea's semiconductor-manufacturing base and AI-semiconductor/PIM ambitions in [EVD-KOR-001](EVD-KOR-001.md) — enabling, and well-evidenced.
  - *Access to frontier-relevant compute:* national AI strategies "explicitly prioritised AI infrastructure, data, cloud computing and AI semiconductors," and Korea's own strategy frames AI semiconductors/PIM as a domain for potential *global leadership*, not mere access — enabling in direction, though this record does not contain a quantified figure (e.g., GPU counts, national supercomputing capacity, or a dedicated compute programme) comparable to those available for some other cases; flagged as an evidentiary gap, not resolved by inference.
  - *External dependence:* "access to advanced AI compute and critical technologies remains embedded in international supply chains" — acknowledged, but stated in general, unquantified terms (E3/OECD-sourced), without a specific figure for AI-chip/GPU/accelerator dependence and without any direct government admission of an inability to compete in compute assembly.
  - *Overall structural significance:* Korea's domestic semiconductor-manufacturing base positions it, in part, as a *supplier* within the global AI-hardware chain rather than solely a consumer of it — a materially different starting configuration from a country whose AI-hardware access rests solely on imports. Combined with the absence of a specific, quantified, or directly-admitted compute-assembly ceiling, the documented external dependence does not rise to a demonstrated *material constraint* on Korea's overall S1 configuration; it remains an acknowledged qualification within an otherwise enabling configuration.
  - This supports **S1 — Enabling**, integrated with [EVD-KOR-001](EVD-KOR-001.md) via explicit researcher judgment applied to the total evidentiary configuration, not derived mechanically from the presence of dependence alone (Codebook §8). **Korea is not characterised as technologically autonomous**: it has substantial and expanding domestic access to compute and related infrastructure, but remains materially embedded in international frontier-technology supply chains, particularly for advanced accelerators.
- **S2 — Market size/dependence:** a substantial domestic industrial/corporate base and existing AI-deployment ecosystem is enabling, but is qualified by significant external market and supply-chain dependence (21% of imported materials/parts/equipment >50% China-dependent in 2022; export concentration) — integrated as **S2 — Enabling** (not unqualified Strong), consistent with the researcher's own framing that dependence coexists with, rather than negates, the enabling market conditions.

## Notes

The Research Note's bibliography lists these OECD analyses across six separate entries citing three underlying reports (two entries per report in two cases); consolidated into three Source records ([SRC-OECD-2024-001](../01-Sources/SRC-OECD-2024-001.md), [SRC-OECD-2025-001](../01-Sources/SRC-OECD-2025-001.md), [SRC-OECD-2025-002](../01-Sources/SRC-OECD-2025-002.md)) per the "consolidate when sources support the same underlying document" principle, and combined into this single Evidence record since all three establish one integrated market-resources/infrastructure/dependence picture rather than genuinely distinct observations.

**S1 consistency review and researcher confirmation (2026-09-02):** a controlled Stage 1 consistency review compared this S1 = Enabling rating against Brazil/Canada/India/Japan, whose own Research Notes reached S1 = Mixed on structurally similar dependence patterns (domestic infrastructure investment coexisting with acknowledged hardware/supply-chain dependence). The researcher confirmed Korea's Enabling rating stands: the international supply-chain dependence documented above remains a qualification/constraint within the justification, but does not by itself justify Mixed, and no new cross-case threshold equating qualitative supply-chain dependence with Mixed is created. This rating continues to reflect the Research Note's own explicit reasoning, not a mechanical application of a cross-case rule.

**Final evidence-based S1 calibration (2026-09-02), following Singapore's S1 revision to Mixed on new primary-source evidence:** the researcher requested an independent re-verification of whether Korea's Enabling rating remains defensible under Codebook §16 on the existing evidence, distinguishing domestic infrastructure/compute capacity, access to frontier-relevant compute, external dependence, and overall structural significance (see "Constructs supported" above for the four-dimension breakdown). Retained **Enabling**: unlike Singapore's new evidence (a direct, quantified, primary-source government admission of an inability to compete in raw compute assembly, backing a specific compute-securing investment), Korea's existing dependence evidence is general and OECD-sourced (E3), without an equivalent quantified figure or direct government admission specific to AI compute/chips, and is offset by Korea's own semiconductor-manufacturing/AI-chip-leadership ambitions ([EVD-KOR-001](EVD-KOR-001.md)), which position Korea partly as a hardware *supplier* rather than solely a consumer. **Visible uncertainty:** this record does not contain a quantified figure for Korea's AI-specific GPU/accelerator dependence (unlike Japan's ~30% domestic-cloud-market-share figure); should such primary-source evidence become available, this rating should be revisited on its own evidentiary merits, not by cross-case harmonisation.

**Final S1 calibration, closed (2026-09-02):** the quantified evidence anticipated above has since been added to the Research Note and formalised in [EVD-KOR-010](EVD-KOR-010.md) (Gwangju AI Data Center: 880 H100 GPUs; planned Supercomputer No. 6: 8,496 NVIDIA GPUs; an announced target of 260,000+ NVIDIA GPUs; OECD confirmation that the Korean AI-semiconductor market is NVIDIA-led). This quantified evidence strengthens, rather than reverses, the Enabling rating — see EVD-KOR-010 for the final S1 assessment and rationale. Treat Korea S1 as closed for Stage 1 unless genuinely new primary evidence later requires reopening it.
